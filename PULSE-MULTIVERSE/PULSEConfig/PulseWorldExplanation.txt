<h2>The Internet You Thought You Had vs The Internet You Actually Use</h2>
<p>
  For years, the internet taught you a simple story:
  <strong>“Your provider gives you 350 Mbps, so everything should be fast.”</strong>
  It sounds right, but it’s not how your device actually behaves.
</p>

<p>
  Here’s the truth nobody explains:
</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Your provider gives you a 350 Mbps pipe.</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Your phone route only uses 6–12 MB/s of it.</strong></li>
  <li><strong>PulseBand uses 150 MB/s of it.</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>
  This is the difference between the internet you were <em>told</em> you have
  and the internet you actually <em>experience</em>.
</p>

<h3>Why You Only Use 6–12 MB/s on a 350 Mbps Connection</h3>
<p>
  Your device doesn’t use the full pipe because the phone route is inefficient.
  It’s slowed down by:
</p>

<ul>
  <li>small TCP windows</li>
  <li>OS-level throttling</li>
  <li>background timers</li>
  <li>latency stalls</li>
  <li>micro-pauses in routing</li>
  <li>legacy scheduling</li>
</ul>

<p>
  So even though your provider gives you 350 Mbps, your apps often behave like
  they’re running on 8 MB/s. That’s why pages feel slow even when your speed test
  looks fast.
</p>

<h3>What PulseBand Does Differently</h3>
<p>
  PulseBand doesn’t use the phone route. It uses a mesh route designed to keep
  your device in a high-efficiency state. That’s why PulseBand shows:
</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>150 MB/s</strong> instead of <strong>8 MB/s</strong></li>
  <li><strong>0.002s</strong> reaction time instead of <strong>0.34s</strong></li>
  <li><strong>17.60×</strong> burst advantage</li>
  <li><strong>9.99×</strong> sustained advantage</li>
</ul>

<p>
  PulseBand isn’t changing your internet provider. It’s changing how your device
  <em>uses</em> the network. It’s the difference between a child route and an
  evolved route.
</p>

<h2>Why Phone Bars Don’t Match PulseBand Bars</h2>
<p>
  Before we go deeper, we need to clear up one of the biggest misconceptions:
  <strong>the difference between Phone Bars, PulseBand Bars, and Phone Bars
  (as PulseBand sees them).</strong>
</p>

<p>
  Your device has <strong>Actual Phone Bars</strong> — the ones shown on your
  phone’s status bar. These come from the modem’s RF strength (RSRP, RSRQ, SINR).
  But carriers <em>inflate</em> these bars so your device “looks” like it has
  strong signal even when the network is congested or throttled.
</p>

<p>
  PulseBand shows two different bar systems:
</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>PulseBand Bars</strong> — actual performance bars</li>
  <li><strong>Phone Bars (PulseBand View)</strong> — PulseBand’s interpretation
      of your phone’s usable performance</li>
</ul>

<p>
  This is why you might see:
</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Actual Phone Bars:</strong> 4/4</li>
  <li><strong>Phone Bars (PulseBand View):</strong> 2/4</li>
  <li><strong>PulseBand Bars:</strong> 2/4</li>
</ul>

<p>
  Your phone shows 4 bars because the RF signal is strong. PulseBand shows 2 bars
  because the <em>network performance</em> is weak. These aren’t contradictions —
  they’re different layers of reality.
</p>

<p class="section-note">
  <strong>In simple terms:</strong><br>
  Actual Phone Bars = “How strong is the radio?”<br>
  Phone Bars (PulseBand View) = “How usable is that radio?”<br>
  PulseBand Bars = “How fast can your device actually move?”
</p>

<h2>The Pulse Behind Every Download: Why Reaction Time Determines Speed</h2>

<p class="section-note">
  <strong>What “Real‑Time Pace” Actually Means:</strong><br>
  Real‑Time Pace is how fast your device can react to a single moment of work —
  not a download, not a file, but the tiny micro‑operations that make everything
  feel instant or sluggish. These micro‑windows are the heartbeat of your device.
</p>

<p>
  When PulseBand shows <strong>0.002s</strong> and the phone route shows
  <strong>0.34s</strong>, it means PulseBand can respond to a request
  <em>170× faster</em> in real time. This matters because every download,
  every page load, every stream, and every app action is made of thousands of
  these micro‑operations.
</p>

<p>
  A download isn’t one big action — it’s millions of tiny “send → wait → receive”
  cycles. If each cycle is slow, the whole download is slow. If each cycle is fast,
  the whole download accelerates.
</p>

<p>
  PulseBand’s 0.002s reaction time means each micro‑operation completes almost
  instantly, so downloads ramp up faster, stay faster, and recover from stalls
  faster. The phone route’s 0.34s reaction time means each micro‑operation is
  delayed, throttled, and paced by legacy scheduling.
</p>

<p class="section-note">
  <strong>In simple terms:</strong><br>
  Real‑Time Pace is the “speed of each heartbeat.” Faster heartbeats mean faster
  downloads, faster page loads, faster streams, and faster everything.
</p>
